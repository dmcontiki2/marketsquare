First, the number that decides whether this works

Probed live · 9 Sep 2026

I asked the live site for every service listing it has, in the whole country, before building anything. GET /listings?category=services returns two: a certified electrician in Pretoria and a garden service in Lynnwood. Both are seeded examples. There is not one cleaner, housekeeper, laundry or ironing listing on TrustSquare today.

That is the argument for this BOT rather than against it. When the shelves are this empty the only question that matters is which shelf fills fastest, and housekeeping is the one where the supply already exists, already has a phone, and already has somebody who will vouch for her.

Your entry, and what it actually costs her to get listed

You said “give the minimum details and be listed — that is the entry”. The app already decides what minimum means, and I used its real arithmetic rather than inventing one. For a service listing, _import_quality_score() splits fifty points between what the job is and a description of fifteen words or more, then adds six for a real price and four for a suburb.

What she givesPointsNote
“I clean houses”25The trade. One sentence.
A description, 15+ words25 The BOT writes this for her out of her answers. This is the point of the whole thing.
A day rate6“Ask me” scores nothing.
Her suburb4One suburb only — see the gap below.
First photo10Then +8 each, capped at 40.

So she is listed at 60 out of 100 after four spoken answers and no photograph at all. She never sees a form. The fifteen-word description — the single thing most likely to make somebody abandon a listing, and worth a quarter of the score — is the thing she never has to write.

Same rule as the Sell It BOT: the scorer here is a faithful copy for the prototype. If it ships, the BOT asks the server for the score. One scorer, never two.

The employer link is the best idea in your message

You wrote it as a convenience — she gives her employer a link so they can book her. It is much more than that, and I built it as the more valuable thing.

1 · It solves the cold start, which nothing else does

Every marketplace dies at the same place: the first hundred sellers nobody has any reason to trust. TrustSquare’s answer is the Trust Score, but a score has to be built out of something. A housekeeper arrives already carrying her evidence — a person who has employed her for three years, who she can reach today, and who has to do exactly one thing: tap yes.

That is the Verified Evidence Ladder working the way it was meant to: a claim, and then a person outside the platform standing behind the claim.

2 · It recruits the demand side for free

Her employer taps the link, sees a clean page, and discovers that the person they already trust is on a marketplace where they could also find a gardener, a tutor or an electrician. Every worker who registers brings one to three middle-class households onto the platform, at no acquisition cost, with a warm reason to be there. No campaign does that.

3 · It fits the money model exactly, with nothing new to build

  • Listing is free — RUL-108 already un-gated the listing BOT for every tier, Free included.
  • Her existing employer books her free. There is nothing to introduce: they already know each other.
  • A stranger asking for her open Wednesday is a real introduction and costs 1 Tuppence. Fixed, not a percentage.
  • Nothing goes through the till but Tuppence. Wages are settled between them, exactly as they are today.

That last point is not a small thing. The moment a platform touches a domestic worker’s wages it becomes an employment agency, with everything that follows. An introduction service does not.

The weekly openings and her area — and the hole they fall into

Both of your last two requirements work in the prototype. Neither exists in the app, and neither would score or search if it did. That is the gap this BOT found.

What you asked forToday
Her openings on a weekly schedule Nothing there
Listings have no concept of a recurring free day. The two seeded service listings say “weekday availability” in their description text — which no filter can read. For a housekeeper the free day is the product.
Her area clearly visible One suburb, worth 4 points
The score holds a single suburb. A housekeeper has two geographies that both matter: where she lives and the five suburbs her taxi route reaches. A buyer in Faerie Glen cannot find her by searching Mamelodi.

What I would build, and it is small

Two fields on a service listing: open_days (seven booleans) and serves_suburbs (a list). Then one search filter — “housekeeping, Menlyn, Wednesday” — which is the only search anybody looking for a cleaner ever runs. Without those two fields this BOT produces beautiful listings that nobody can find, which is the expensive kind of half-built.

Flagged, not changed — it touches the listing schema and the search filter, and this is launch month.

The one thing I would gate, and I would argue hard for it

Letting a stranger into your empty house is the highest-trust transaction this marketplace will ever carry. It is not a bakkie. If TrustSquare gets this wrong once, in public, the damage is not to a listing — it is to the name.

The rule I built into the prototype: a housekeeper is listed immediately, but until at least one employer confirmation or an ID check lands, only people she has sent her own link to can contact her. She loses nothing — her existing employers are exactly who she wants to reach on day one. Strangers see her only once somebody real has vouched.

This also protects her, which matters more and is easier to forget. A listing that puts a woman’s open days and her suburb in front of anybody at all, with no accountability on the other side, is not a service to her.

It is the same shape as the publish floor I flagged on the Sell It BOT — a score alone should not be the only gate — but here the argument is much stronger.

The wage floor, which the BOT checks out loud

From 1 March 2026 the national minimum wage is R30.23 for each ordinary hour, and it applies to domestic workers on the same footing as everyone else. Because the Basic Conditions of Employment Act pays a worker for four hours even on a shorter shift, the practical floor for a short day is R120.92.

So the BOT does the arithmetic in front of her. R350 for an eight-hour day is R43.75 an hour and it says so. A rate below the floor gets one sentence — “that works out under the legal minimum; shall we put it at R242?” — and it will not publish it.

Why this is a feature and not a compliance chore: it is the clearest possible signal about what kind of marketplace this is, it protects the platform from being the place where underpayment is arranged, and it is the sort of thing a worker tells her friends about. It costs one comparison.

Two things about the phone that make this cheap

She talks, in her own language, for nothing

The phone does the listening and the speaking — webkitSpeechRecognition and speechSynthesis, no licence and no per-minute bill, exactly as the Collector BOT established. The language buttons in the prototype are real: en-ZA, af-ZA and zu-ZA are recognised by Android Chrome.

Unverified Sepedi, Xitsonga and isiXhosa are not reliably offered, and the microphone in installed (standalone) mode on iPhone has always been the flaky one. Both need a real phone in a real hand before anybody promises them. That is a twenty-minute test, not a project.

They turned out to be a better answer than the housekeeping photos I was going to ask you to pay for. “No none photos” — that is now a rule, not a nudge

I flagged this on the Sell It BOT as a hole and left it for you: the score lets a listing with no photograph at all clear the 50-point bar, because the facts alone are worth 60. You have just closed it. One photo is now a floor under publishing here — the button says “one photo first” and will not go until there is one.

It matters more in this category than anywhere else. Nobody lets a stranger into their empty house off a listing with no picture, and a shelf of pictureless listings makes the whole marketplace look abandoned.

Recorded as your ruling. The same floor belongs on the Sell It BOT and, I would argue, on the publish gate itself — but moving the live gate is a launch-month change, so it is written down rather than done.

Moreleta Park — you found a real defect, and it was worse than it looked

The first version matched a hard list of fifteen suburbs. Moreleta Park was on it, but three things were broken around it:

  • It never said the name back. It stored your suburb and moved straight to the next question, so from your side it looked like it had ignored you. It now answers “Moreleta Park. Got it — that is your area on the listing.”
  • Anything off the list failed silently. Mabopane, Midrand, Olievenhoutbosch — all rejected. A list can never be long enough; there are thousands of suburbs.
  • Speech made it worse. The phone hears “Morelia Park” and an exact match fails, which is the spoken version of the same bug.

What it does now

The list is only a spelling aid, not the gate. Anything sitting after stay in, live in, work in, from or near is accepted as a place whether we have heard of it or not, and a near-miss is snapped to the closest name we know — so “Morelia Park” becomes Moreleta Park. Saying just the name on its own works too. And the verb decides the meaning: stay makes it her home, work makes it a suburb she travels to, in the same sentence if she says both.

And a second thing fell out of it, which is worth more than the bug

The spelling aid is now the app’s own assets/suburbs_seed.json — 119 suburbs across twelve cities. Reading it showed something: it contains almost no townships. Arcadia, Brooklyn, Waterkloof, Centurion — but not Mamelodi, not Soshanguve, not Mabopane, not Tembisa, not Khayelitsha, not Umlazi.

That is not a small omission for this BOT. The seed lists where the work is, not where she lives. Every housekeeper on the platform would have hit it. I have added the townships to this prototype’s list, but the real fix is the seed file itself, and it matters for search, for the city launcher and for anything else that reads it.

Tested written and spoken-style: Moreleta Park, Mabopane, Midrand, Olievenhoutbosch, “Morelia Park”, and the name on its own with nothing around it. Nine cases, all passing.

Where this stands

Design only. Nothing wired, no flag, no line in the deploy manifest, not launch scope — the same footing as the Collector and the Sell It BOTs. Three BOTs now exist as working pictures.

Two decisions I made rather than handing back:

  • The colour. You asked for green and green was already spoken for by the Sell It BOT (#2F8D5C). Home Help takes a brighter jade, #16A97C — it reads as clean rather than as money. Sell It moves to TrustSquare gold when it gets built; it has no icons yet, so that costs nothing.
  • Where the circle lives. Both places, and they are the same circle. On the phone home screen it is the BOT’s own icon. Inside the app it is one green circle in the Services category, sitting where a housekeeper would look for it.

What is genuinely yours to say: whether housekeeping goes ahead of the Collector and the Sell It BOTs in the build order. My answer is yes, and the reason is on the first panel — it is the only one of the three where the supply is already out there, already has a phone, and arrives with somebody willing to vouch for her.
