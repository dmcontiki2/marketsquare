RUL-131 · RUL-132 — the lead, and whose convenience wins

MarketSquare · ruled 14 September 2026, amended the same day · what it costs to build, by piece

The agency pays. One agency holds the seller's lead at a time — what is scarce is not his requests but who holds the lead, which is exactly what makes it worth buying. The window ends on his word or on silence, never only on a clock. Queue order is the three scores, never payment. A decline releases the hold in full, or agencies stop bidding.
RUL-132, amending the above: the user's ease outranks our tidiness. Vary the flow freely; never fork the consent model. One agency at a time is a property rule only — a traveller wanting three B&B quotes gets three, and guides, tour agents and B&Bs each get the flow their trade needs. What never varies: connected parties only, each side's own switch, the sender's name on every buzz, a decline costing the decliner nothing, no per-message cost. And the user sees one thing called Buzz.

The one that was not free — now settled

Quick Listing had exactly one outcome: five taps produce a draft listing. A private seller wanting an agent is asking for an introduction instead, which needed a second hand-over target. RUL-132(g) settles it — "one server, one rulebook" was always about one rulebook and one database, never one destination.

Rejected: one free referral, then the seller pays Seller gets the first agency free and pays from the second. David's own instinct called it an exception-type requirement, and it is: it would put two payers in one lane and bill the side we most need at the moment they are most engaged. The one-at-a-time rule removes the need for it entirely, because it limits the lead rather than the person.
The bill, stated plainly Every extra flow is maintenance on one founder. Worth paying when the user is genuinely doing something different; not worth paying because a category exists. That is the trade David chose, with his eyes open.
Named, not parked The window's length, the non-contact trigger's duration, and the abuse gate that free-for-the-seller implies — though burn-on-delivery already answers most of that last one, since a fake seller who never accepts costs the agency nothing.
